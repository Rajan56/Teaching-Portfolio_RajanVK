01 · Teaching philosophy

Four principles I teach by

The first three reflect established pedagogical practice. The fourth comes from my own experience as a learner: real understanding grows from inquiry and observation, and it needs freedom from fear.

1

Clarity

A complex idea should be made simple without losing its meaning. A clear explanation gives students confidence, shows them the structure of a topic, and lets them work with it on their own.

2

Relevance

Students learn best when they see why knowledge matters. I bring real industrial problems and my research on digital twins, AI and performance management into the classroom.

3

Student-centred learning

Students arrive with different backgrounds and strengths. My role is to guide, not to dominate: patient tutoring, constructive feedback and tasks they own.

4

Insight, not accumulation

Learning should not be driven by fear, comparison or pressure. Fear suppresses curiosity. Safety makes exploration possible.

What the fourth principle looks like in practice

Inquiry over memorisation

I invite students to question assumptions and examine contradictions instead of reproducing information.

No fear-based learning

Standards matter, and so does psychological safety. A mistake is part of learning, not a verdict on ability.

Awareness of one's own thinking

Students reflect on how they approach a problem, read data and build an argument, so they learn to correct themselves.

Dialogue as method

Dialogue is shared exploration, not a transfer of authority. I ask before I tell.

Two classrooms, one method

In Nepal I taught fifteen-year-olds with a water bottle and a rooftop. In Finland I mentor university teams working with company managers. The setting changed completely. The method did not change: start from a question the students care about, let them predict, let them test, and only then explain.

How I give feedback

  • Break a complex task into steps a student can act on.
  • Explain what to improve and why it matters.
  • Name strengths, not only errors.
  • Offer another method or viewpoint where it helps.
  • Ask students to reflect on their own reasoning.

Background that shapes my teaching

B.Sc. in Chemistry (Tribhuvan University), then bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees in Industrial Engineering and Management in Finland (HAMK and LUT). Science, engineering and management in one path.

First-year students remember what they have done, not what they have heard, so I built the sessions around interaction. Concept visualisation · process thinking

Find the bottleneck

A line of four stations. Students first guess how many products per hour the line can finish, then change the capacities and watch. The lesson arrives without a formula: a process is only as fast as its slowest step, and improving anything else is wasted effort.

Line output: 8 products per hour. Bottleneck: Assembly.

Pink dots are work waiting in front of a station. Black dots are products moving along the line.
Self-discovery · teamwork

Which team roles come naturally to you?

Before the first group assignment, students rated a few statements about themselves and saw which team roles suit them, and where in industry those strengths are valued. Groups then compared profiles and noticed which roles their team was missing. Move the sliders from "not like me" to "very much like me".

An informal classroom reflection inspired by Belbin's nine team roles. It is not the official Belbin assessment and gives no diagnosis, only a starting point for discussion.

Study skills · time management

Urgent or important?

New students often treat everything that is loud as important. In this short game they sort typical tasks of a student week into four quadrants and compare their choices with mine. The discussion afterwards is the real lesson: which quadrant decides a degree?

Read the task on the right and choose a quadrant.

Why tools and games

A first-year group is mixed in background and confidence. A game gives everyone the same starting point, and a tool that says something about the student personally earns attention that a slide cannot.

What I did in the course

Delivered lectures, tutored students individually through assignments and project work, assessed their work and gave feedback. I also assisted and graded on other courses of the department.

04 · Course design

A constructively aligned redesign of a first-year course

As my course planning task in LUT's University Pedagogy studies (submitted 15 May 2026), I redesigned Introduction to Industrial Engineering and Management, a compulsory 3 ECTS blended course for first-year bachelor students. The content was sound. The weakness was coherence: students met the themes as a list of separate tasks. The redesign aligns outcomes, activities and assessment. Select an outcome to see how it is taught and assessed.

Constructive alignment

Outcomes, activities and assessment match, so students know what is worth their time (Biggs & Tang, 2011).

Active learning

Low-threshold, clearly guided activity instead of listening only (Prince, 2004).

Scaffolding

Templates and worked examples early on, with support withdrawn as confidence grows (van de Pol et al., 2010).

Formative feedback

Brief, criteria-linked feedback built into each module (Nicol & Macfarlane-Dick, 2006).

Each module follows a before, during and after rhythm in the blended environment. Assessment instructions state plainly which uses of generative AI are acceptable, and students declare any AI assistance.

05 · Mentoring · Lahti Venture Program

Real companies, real problems, mixed teams

The Lahti Venture Program is an industry collaboration course (6 ECTS) at LUT University in which student teams solve a challenge set by a company. Its teams bring together students from different disciplines, so the first lesson is often how to work with someone who thinks differently. I mentored teams in the programme from 2022 to 2026.

Example challenge

Kempower: attracting and keeping Generation Z talent

Kempower, a Finnish maker of electric vehicle charging systems, asked how it could attract and retain employees from Generation Z. The students were themselves that generation, so I began with their own expectations of an employer and turned those into a first hypothesis. From there the team worked in four steps.

  1. Listen. Surveys and interviews with students and young professionals.
  2. Compare. How other technology companies appeal to young talent.
  3. Analyse. Themes, strengths and gaps: purpose, growth, flexibility and openness.
  4. Propose. An employer value proposition for Generation Z, with steps for recruitment, onboarding and retention.

My belief is that teaching should be student-oriented, and mentoring makes that concrete. The ideas belong to the students. My task is to protect the space in which those ideas can appear, and then to give them structure.

06 · School science teaching in Nepal · demonstrations you can try

Predict. Observe. Explain.

Before coming to Finland I taught science and chemistry at secondary level for five years. These two simulations recreate how I taught. Each follows the same rhythm: students commit to a prediction, watch what really happens, and then build the explanation together.

Physics · Class 10 · Nepal

"If Einstein is right, the water will stop."

I took my class to the roof with a plastic bottle full of water and a few holes near its base. While I held it, water streamed out. Then I told them: if Einstein is right, the streams will stop the moment I let go. Everyone had to predict before I released it.

Step 1 · Your prediction
Step 2 · Observe
Step 3 · Explain

While the bottle is held, the weight of the water above each hole creates pressure, and that pressure pushes water out. In free fall the bottle and the water accelerate downward together. The water no longer presses on what is below it, so nothing drives it through the holes. Inside the falling bottle, gravity seems to have been switched off. That is what Einstein's equivalence principle says: a freely falling frame is locally indistinguishable from one with no gravity at all.

07 · Teaching civil engineering students

One building, four lenses

This is how I would introduce bachelor students in civil engineering to system-level thinking. A single renovation decision is examined through technical, business, environmental and societal lenses, and then through the eyes of different stakeholders. The exercise shows that an engineering decision is also an economic, ecological and social one.

The case

A municipal concrete building from 1974, 4,000 m², structurally sound but with ageing services and poor energy performance. The owner must decide what to do with it.

Whose decision is it?

Illustrative teaching values per m², chosen to be plausible in order of magnitude. They are not design data. The point of the exercise is the reasoning, and students replace these numbers with sourced ones as a course task.

What students practise

Framing a problem before solving it, life-cycle thinking, reading trade-offs, and defending a recommendation to people who value different things.

How it runs in class

Teams first predict the best option. Each team then takes one stakeholder role, sets the weights, and argues its case. A short debrief compares the rankings and asks why they differ.

What it leads to

The same case carries later topics: life-cycle costing, embodied carbon, circular business models, material passports and digital twins of existing buildings.

Design science in a bachelor project

Engineering students want to build something. Design science research gives that instinct a rigorous shape: a real problem, an artefact, and an honest evaluation. Step through how I would guide a student team, using a material passport for a renovation site as the example.

08 · Sustainability in teaching

Three bottom lines in every case

Sustainability is the core of my research, and I do not teach it as a separate chapter. Every case I use is read through the triple bottom line: economic, environmental and social. Below are two teaching designs that grow directly out of my research.

Teaching design 1

The three-column KPI audit

Students take the performance indicators a real organisation reports and sort them into economic, environmental and social columns. The empty spaces are the lesson: what is not measured is not managed. Teams then propose two indicators that would fill the largest gap and justify the data needed. The exercise draws on my doctoral research on performance measurement, smart technologies and sustainability in SMEs.

Teaching design 2

Keep it or rebuild it?

The "one building, four lenses" exercise above is a sustainability lesson in disguise. Extending the life of an existing structure preserves embodied carbon and capital, and students discover with their own numbers when that holds and when it does not. It draws on my work on digital twins and life extension of ageing assets.

In the first-year course

Sustainability and ethics as an outcome

In my redesign of Introduction to Industrial Engineering and Management, sustainability is written into the intended learning outcomes and assessed through a reflection that links a company visit to the role of the engineer.
